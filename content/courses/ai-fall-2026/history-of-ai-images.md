# A History of AI Image Generation

Every image-generating model does the same basic thing: it looks at a pile of pictures, builds an internal statistical model of what those pictures have in common, and then produces new samples from that model. What changes over the decades is *how* the model is built, *how much* it can hold, and *how* we steer it.

This timeline starts in 1987, before neural networks were fashionable, and runs to the present. A few things to watch for as you scroll:

- **Latent space** shows up in 1987 and never leaves. Nearly every model here learns a compressed space of "possible images" and generates by picking a point in it.
- **Resolution and fidelity climb slowly, then explode.** For twenty-five years the state of the art was a 28×28 blurry digit. Between 2014 and 2022 it went to photorealistic megapixels.
- **Control is the real story.** Early models generated *something*; the modern history of the field is the history of learning to tell a model *what* to make — first with class labels, then with sketches, then with sentences.
- **Artists were never downstream of this.** Deep Dream, style transfer, pix2pix, and CLIP-guided synthesis were all adopted, misused, and pushed by artists within weeks of release, and in the CLIP era artists were writing the code.

> [!note] Before machine learning
> Rule-based image generation is much older. Harold Cohen's **AARON** (begun 1973) drew autonomously for decades; Vera Molnár, Manfred Mohr, and Frieder Nake were making algorithmic images in the 1960s; Jason Salavon's photographic composites (late 1990s) are statistical images made without learning. None of these are machine learning — nothing is *trained* on data — but they establish the questions the models below inherit.

## Era 1 — Statistical and energy-based models (1987–2013)

Small, grayscale, mathematically elegant, and visually humble. These models proved the concept: a machine can learn a distribution over images and sample from it.

| ![[eigenfaces.png\|340]]                   | **1987 / 1991 · Eigenfaces (PCA on images)**<br><br>**Who:** Lawrence Sirovich and Michael Kirby (Brown University, 1987); Matthew Turk and Alex Pentland (MIT Media Lab, 1991).<br><br>**How it works:** Flatten every grayscale face photo into a long vector of pixel values, then run Principal Component Analysis across the whole dataset. The top eigenvectors — the "eigenfaces" — form a basis. Any face in the dataset can be rebuilt as *the average face plus a weighted sum of eigenfaces*. Choose your own weights and you get a face that was never photographed. No neural network, no nonlinearity: this is pure linear algebra.<br><br>**Why it matters:** This is the earliest widely known case of *learning an image space from data and generating new images by moving through it*. The idea that images live on a low-dimensional manifold, and that you can slide along its axes, is invented here and never abandoned. When you drag a slider in Artbreeder in 2019, you are doing what Sirovich and Kirby did in 1987, with a better decoder.<br><br>**Artists:** No canonical eigenface artworks, but the lineage is artistically live. **Jason Salavon**'s averaged composites work on the same statistical logic without training; **Trevor Paglen** and **Kyle McDonald** have made work interrogating the face-recognition apparatus that eigenfaces launched.<br><br>**More examples:** [Wikipedia: Eigenface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenface) · [Turk & Pentland, *Eigenfaces for Recognition* (1991)](https://direct.mit.edu/jocn/article/3/1/71/3025)<br><br>**Try it:** [scikit-learn: Faces dataset decompositions](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/auto_examples/decomposition/plot_faces_decomposition.html) — runnable in a Colab notebook in about ten lines.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                 |
| ![[restricted-boltzmann-machine.png\|340]] | **1985 / 2006 · Boltzmann Machines, RBMs, and Deep Belief Networks**<br><br>**Who:** Geoffrey Hinton and Terry Sejnowski (Boltzmann machine, 1985); Paul Smolensky (the restricted variant, 1986); Hinton, Simon Osindero, and Yee-Whye Teh (Deep Belief Networks, 2006).<br><br>**How it works:** An *energy-based* model. Binary units are wired together; the network assigns an energy to every possible configuration of pixels, and learning lowers the energy of configurations that look like the training data. To generate, you run Gibbs sampling — repeatedly flipping units according to their probabilities — and let the network settle into a low-energy state, which is an image. A Restricted Boltzmann Machine simplifies this into two layers (visible pixels, hidden features) with no within-layer connections, which makes training tractable. Deep Belief Networks stack RBMs and train them greedily, one layer at a time.<br><br>**Why it matters:** The first genuinely *neural* generative image models, and the papers that restarted deep learning after its second winter. Hinton's 2006 demo of a DBN "dreaming" MNIST digits — the network wandering between 0s and 8s on its own — is the ancestor of every latent-space walk video you've seen. It also established the idea that one network can both recognize and generate, which is still the shape of the field.<br><br>**Artists:** Essentially none directly — the outputs were 28×28 binary digits. This entry is here as ancestry, not as a tool.<br><br>**More examples:** [Hinton's original MNIST generation demo](https://www.cs.toronto.edu/~hinton/adi/index.htm) (an old Java applet page, but the recorded output is the point) · [*A Fast Learning Algorithm for Deep Belief Nets* (2006)](https://www.cs.toronto.edu/~hinton/absps/fastnc.pdf)<br><br>**Try it:** [scikit-learn `BernoulliRBM` on digits](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/auto_examples/neural_networks/plot_rbm_logistic_classification.html) — trains on a laptop CPU in under a minute. |
| ![[vae-manifolds.png\|340]]                | **2013 · Variational Autoencoder (VAE)**<br><br>**Who:** Diederik P. Kingma and Max Welling (University of Amsterdam); independently and simultaneously Danilo Rezende, Shakir Mohamed, and Daan Wierstra (DeepMind).<br><br>**How it works:** An autoencoder squeezes an image through a narrow bottleneck and reconstructs it. A *variational* autoencoder adds a crucial constraint: the bottleneck must be a probability distribution shaped like a smooth Gaussian blob. That forces the latent space to be continuous and densely filled — every point decodes to *something* plausible, and points near each other decode to similar images. The "reparameterization trick" is what makes this trainable by ordinary backpropagation.<br><br>**Why it matters:** VAEs gave us the *well-behaved* latent space. GANs would soon beat them on image sharpness, but the VAE's clean, interpolatable, mathematically principled latent space is what modern diffusion models actually run inside — Stable Diffusion's "latent" is a VAE latent. This is the quiet workhorse of the whole timeline.<br><br>**Artists:** Rarely used alone for finished work — VAE output is famously blurry, because averaging over uncertainty produces soft images. But VAE-style latent interpolation is the grammar of latent-space video art, and VAEs are the compression layer inside almost every model below from 2021 onward.<br><br>**More examples:** [*Auto-Encoding Variational Bayes* (arXiv 1312.6114)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1312.6114) · [Kingma & Welling, *An Introduction to Variational Autoencoders*](https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.02691)<br><br>**Try it:** [TensorFlow CVAE tutorial (Colab)](https://colab.research.google.com/github/tensorflow/docs/blob/master/site/en/tutorials/generative/cvae.ipynb) — trains on MNIST in a few minutes and prints the manifold grid shown here.                                                                                                                                                                 |

## Era 2 — The adversarial breakthrough (2014–2016)

Two networks fight; images get sharp. This is where machine-generated images stopped looking like statistics and started looking like pictures.

| Example | Model |
|---|---|
| ![[gan-2014-samples.png\|340]] | **2014 · Generative Adversarial Network (GAN)**<br><br>**Who:** Ian Goodfellow, Jean Pouget-Abadie, Mehdi Mirza, Bing Xu, David Warde-Farley, Sherjil Ozair, Aaron Courville, and Yoshua Bengio (Université de Montréal). Famously sketched out in a bar argument and coded the same night.<br><br>**How it works:** Two networks trained against each other. The **generator** turns random noise into an image. The **discriminator** looks at an image and guesses whether it came from the training set or from the generator. Each one's loss is the other's gain. The generator never sees a real image directly — it only ever learns from the discriminator's gradient, i.e. from *what would have made that more convincing*. At equilibrium the discriminator is reduced to guessing.<br><br>**Why it matters:** The single most important idea in the first decade of neural image generation. Because the objective is "fool a classifier" rather than "minimize pixel error," GANs escaped the blurriness that plagued VAEs and produced genuinely sharp images. Yann LeCun called adversarial training the most interesting idea in ML in twenty years. Everything from 2014 to roughly 2021 is downstream of this paper.<br><br>**Artists:** The whole first generation of neural artists — **Mario Klingemann**, **Anna Ridler**, **Helena Sarin**, **Robbie Barrat**, **Memo Akten**, **Sofia Crespo**, **Jake Elwes**, **Refik Anadol** — built their practices on GANs.<br><br>**More examples:** [*Generative Adversarial Nets* (arXiv 1406.2661)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1406.2661)<br><br>**Try it:** [TensorFlow DCGAN tutorial (Colab)](https://colab.research.google.com/github/tensorflow/docs/blob/master/site/en/tutorials/generative/dcgan.ipynb) — the closest thing to a "hello world" GAN. |
| ![[draw-samples.jpg\|340]] | **2015 · DRAW (Deep Recurrent Attentive Writer)**<br><br>**Who:** Karol Gregor, Ivo Danihelka, Alex Graves, Danilo Rezende, and Daan Wierstra (Google DeepMind).<br><br>**How it works:** A recurrent VAE with an attention mechanism, which generates an image *over time* rather than all at once. At each timestep the network decides where to look and what to add, writing patches onto a canvas — a sequence of glances and strokes rather than a single forward pass. The attention window is a differentiable array of Gaussian filters, so "where to look" is learned, not scripted.<br><br>**Why it matters:** The first model to generate images the way a person draws: iteratively, attending to one region at a time, refining. It was also the first model to convincingly generate multi-object scenes (street-number photographs) rather than single centered objects. Conceptually it is the ancestor of every iterative-refinement generator, diffusion included.<br><br>**Artists:** Little direct artistic use, but DRAW is the paper most often cited when people argue that generation should be a *process* an artist can intervene in rather than a button.<br><br>**More examples:** [*DRAW: A Recurrent Neural Network For Image Generation* (arXiv 1502.04623)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1502.04623) · [DeepMind's generation video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Zt-7MI9eKEo)<br><br>**Try it:** No maintained hosted demo; the [eriklindernoren PyTorch-GAN and related reimplementations](https://github.com/ericjang/draw) run in Colab with light editing. |
| ![[aligndraw-stop-sign.png\|340]] | **2015 · alignDRAW — the first text-to-image model**<br><br>**Who:** Elman Mansimov, Emilio Parisotto, Jimmy Lei Ba, and Ruslan Salakhutdinov (University of Toronto).<br><br>**How it works:** DRAW, conditioned on a caption. A bidirectional RNN reads the sentence into a sequence of word vectors; at every drawing timestep the generator *aligns* — attends over those word vectors and decides which words matter for the patch it is about to paint. Trained on Microsoft COCO captions.<br><br>**Why it matters:** This is the origin of text-to-image. The authors' headline test was a caption no photograph in the dataset could contain — *"A stop sign is flying in blue skies"* — specifically to prove the model was composing novel scenes rather than retrieving memorized ones. The images are 32×32 smudges. The entire argument for prompting as a creative interface is already fully formed here, seven years before DALL·E 2. The same stop-sign prompt is still used as a benchmark today (see the 2025 entry at the bottom of this page).<br><br>**Artists:** None at the time — the outputs were unusable. Its importance is conceptual.<br><br>**More examples:** [Project page with generated samples](http://www.cs.toronto.edu/~emansim/cap2im.html) · [*Generating Images from Captions with Attention* (arXiv 1511.02793)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1511.02793)<br><br>**Try it:** [Original Theano code on GitHub](https://github.com/emansim/text2image) (historical; expect to fight the dependencies). |
| ![[diffusion-2015-samples.png\|340]] | **2015 · Diffusion Probabilistic Models**<br><br>**Who:** Jascha Sohl-Dickstein, Eric Weiss, Niru Maheswaranathan, and Surya Ganguli (Stanford / UC Berkeley).<br><br>**How it works:** Borrowed directly from non-equilibrium thermodynamics. Take a training image and destroy it gradually — add a little Gaussian noise, then a little more, hundreds of times, until it is pure static. That *forward* process is fixed and requires no learning. Then train a network to reverse a single step of it. To generate, start from pure noise and run the learned reverse steps until an image condenses out.<br><br>**Why it matters:** This is the paper that everything after 2020 runs on, and it sat almost unnoticed for five years because the samples were poor and sampling was ruinously slow. It is the best example in this timeline of an idea arriving before the engineering that makes it work. Note how bad the CIFAR-10 samples here look compared to a GAN of the same year.<br><br>**Artists:** None in 2015. By 2022 essentially all of them.<br><br>**More examples:** [*Deep Unsupervised Learning using Nonequilibrium Thermodynamics* (arXiv 1503.03585)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1503.03585)<br><br>**Try it:** See the 2020 DDPM entry below — that is the version worth running. |
| ![[deepdream-arcimboldo.gif\|340]] | **2015 · Deep Dream (Inceptionism)**<br><br>**Who:** Alexander Mordvintsev, with Christopher Olah and Mike Tyka (Google).<br><br>**How it works:** Not a generative model at all — a *classifier run backwards*. Take a trained image-recognition network (Inception, trained on ImageNet), pick a layer, and instead of adjusting the network's weights, adjust the **input image** by gradient ascent to maximize whatever that layer is already detecting. Whatever the network faintly sees, it is told to see harder. Run it at multiple scales, repeatedly, and dogs and eyes and pagodas bloom out of clouds and static.<br><br>**Why it matters:** The first machine-generated images to go genuinely viral, and the moment the general public formed a mental image of "what AI art looks like." It was also a landmark in interpretability research — it was made to answer *what is this network actually looking for?* — and the answer turned out to be beautiful. Released as open code, so anyone could run it within days.<br><br>**Artists:** **Mike Tyka** (a co-author who is also a sculptor and printmaker; his large-format Deep Dream prints were among the first neural works shown in galleries), **Memo Akten**, **Gene Kogan**, **Kyle McDonald**. Deep Dream is also the reason "Google's AI has a dog problem" was a 2015 news cycle — the ImageNet class list is roughly 12% dog breeds.<br><br>**More examples:** [Google Research: *Inceptionism*](https://research.google/blog/inceptionism-going-deeper-into-neural-networks/) · [Wikimedia Commons: Deep Dream images](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Deep_Dream_images)<br><br>**Try it:** [TensorFlow Deep Dream tutorial (Colab)](https://colab.research.google.com/github/tensorflow/docs/blob/master/site/en/tutorials/generative/deepdream.ipynb) · [Deep Dream Generator (web, upload an image)](https://deepdreamgenerator.com/) |
| ![[neural-style-transfer-wales.jpg\|340]] | **2015 · Neural Style Transfer**<br><br>**Who:** Leon Gatys, Alexander Ecker, and Matthias Bethge (University of Tübingen).<br><br>**How it works:** The key insight is that a trained CNN separates *content* from *style* on its own. Content lives in the raw activations of deeper layers (what objects are where); style lives in the **Gram matrices** — the correlations *between* feature channels — which describe texture and color relationships while discarding spatial arrangement. So: start from noise or from the content photo, and optimize the pixels to simultaneously match one image's deep activations and another image's Gram matrices.<br><br>**Why it matters:** The first neural technique with an obvious, legible creative use, and the first to become a consumer product — Prisma launched in 2016 and hit tens of millions of downloads. It also seeded the first big public argument about machine learning and artistic authorship, since the style being transferred belongs to someone.<br><br>**Artists:** **Gene Kogan** (whose tutorials taught a generation of artists this technique), **Kyle McDonald**, **Memo Akten**, **Mario Klingemann**. Later folded into countless video works via frame-by-frame stylization.<br><br>**More examples:** [*A Neural Algorithm of Artistic Style* (arXiv 1508.06576)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1508.06576) · [Justin Johnson's `neural-style` gallery](https://github.com/jcjohnson/neural-style)<br><br>**Try it:** [TensorFlow style transfer tutorial (Colab)](https://colab.research.google.com/github/tensorflow/docs/blob/master/site/en/tutorials/generative/style_transfer.ipynb) · [ml5.js style transfer](https://ml5js.org/) in the browser with p5.js |
| ![[dcgan-bedrooms.png\|340]]<br>![[dcgan-face-arithmetic.jpg\|340]] | **2015 / 2016 · DCGAN (Deep Convolutional GAN)**<br><br>**Who:** Alec Radford, Luke Metz, and Soumith Chintala (indico Research and Facebook AI Research).<br><br>**How it works:** GANs as originally published were notoriously unstable. DCGAN is the recipe that made them work: all-convolutional architectures with no pooling layers, batch normalization in both networks, ReLU in the generator and LeakyReLU in the discriminator, and strided transposed convolutions for upsampling. The generator turns a 100-dimensional noise vector into a 64×64 image.<br><br>**Why it matters:** The paper that turned GANs from a beautiful idea into a tool anyone could use, and the architecture nearly every artist-facing GAN for the next three years was built on. It also demonstrated **latent vector arithmetic** — the famous "smiling woman − neutral woman + neutral man = smiling man" figure shown here — which proved the latent space had learned meaningful, manipulable semantic directions rather than arbitrary coordinates.<br><br>**Artists:** **Robbie Barrat** (whose nude-portrait and landscape GANs, trained as a teenager and released openly, were enormously influential), the **Obvious** collective — whose *Portrait of Edmond de Belamy* used Barrat's published code and sold at Christie's in 2018 for $432,500, the first AI-generated work at a major auction house — plus **Helena Sarin**, **Anna Ridler**, and **Mario Klingemann**.<br><br>**More examples:** [*Unsupervised Representation Learning with Deep Convolutional GANs* (arXiv 1511.06434)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1511.06434) · [Christie's lot page for *Edmond de Belamy*](https://www.christies.com/en/lot/lot-6166184)<br><br>**Try it:** [TensorFlow DCGAN tutorial (Colab)](https://colab.research.google.com/github/tensorflow/docs/blob/master/site/en/tutorials/generative/dcgan.ipynb) · [PyTorch DCGAN tutorial](https://pytorch.org/tutorials/beginner/dcgan_faces_tutorial.html) |
| ![[pixelcnn-samples.jpg\|340]] | **2016 · PixelRNN and PixelCNN**<br><br>**Who:** Aäron van den Oord, Nal Kalchbrenner, and Koray Kavukcuoglu (Google DeepMind).<br><br>**How it works:** The purely **autoregressive** approach. Treat an image as a sequence of pixels in raster order and model it exactly the way a language model models text: predict pixel *n* given all pixels before it, as a full probability distribution over the 256 possible values per channel. Masked convolutions ensure a pixel can only see its predecessors. Generation is literally one pixel at a time.<br><br>**Why it matters:** The road not taken — until it was. Autoregressive models gave the best *likelihoods* of any 2016 generative model and produced no blur and no mode collapse, but generating a 64×64 image required thousands of sequential forward passes. The approach was sidelined for speed, then returned decisively: DALL·E 1 is autoregressive, and by 2025 frontier models had come all the way back to it.<br><br>**Artists:** Little direct use — too slow to iterate with. Its descendants are everywhere.<br><br>**More examples:** [*Pixel Recurrent Neural Networks* (arXiv 1601.06759)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1601.06759) · [*Conditional Image Generation with PixelCNN Decoders* (arXiv 1606.05328)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1606.05328)<br><br>**Try it:** [OpenAI's PixelCNN++ code](https://github.com/openai/pixel-cnn) · [Keras PixelCNN example](https://keras.io/examples/generative/pixelcnn/) |
| ![[pix2pix-teaser.png\|340]] | **2016 · pix2pix (Conditional Adversarial Networks)**<br><br>**Who:** Phillip Isola, Jun-Yan Zhu, Tinghui Zhou, and Alexei A. Efros (UC Berkeley / BAIR).<br><br>**How it works:** Image-to-image translation with a **conditional** GAN. Instead of noise in and image out, you feed the generator an input image — a sketch, a segmentation map, an edge map, a satellite photo — and it produces a corresponding output image. The generator is a U-Net (so fine detail can skip past the bottleneck); the discriminator is a "PatchGAN" that judges local patches rather than the whole image, which sharpens texture. The loss combines adversarial loss with an L1 term that keeps the output faithful to the input. Requires **paired** training data.<br><br>**Why it matters:** This is the model that made neural image generation *interactive*. You draw, it responds. For many artists pix2pix was the first machine learning tool that felt like an instrument rather than a slot machine, and it launched a wave of "sketch → thing" web toys, most famously **edges2cats**.<br><br>**Artists:** **Memo Akten** — *Learning to See* (2017) trains pix2pix on one dataset and feeds it a live camera pointed at cloths and cables, so the model hallucinates waves or flowers from whatever it is shown; still the sharpest artwork about model bias ever made with these tools. Also **Mario Klingemann**, **Helena Sarin** (whose work is almost entirely small hand-made-dataset pix2pix and CycleGAN), **Sofia Crespo**, **Anna Ridler**.<br><br>**More examples:** [pix2pix project page](https://phillipi.github.io/pix2pix/) · [Memo Akten, *Learning to See*](https://www.memo.tv/works/learning-to-see/)<br><br>**Try it:** [TensorFlow pix2pix tutorial (Colab)](https://colab.research.google.com/github/tensorflow/docs/blob/master/site/en/tutorials/generative/pix2pix.ipynb) · [ml5.js pix2pix examples](https://ml5js.org/) |
| ![[stackgan-birds.jpg\|340]] | **2016 / 2017 · StackGAN**<br><br>**Who:** Han Zhang, Tao Xu, Hongsheng Li, Shaoting Zhang, Xiaogang Wang, Xiaolei Huang, and Dimitris Metaxas (Rutgers, Lehigh, CUHK, Baidu Research).<br><br>**How it works:** Text-to-image in two stages, because trying to jump straight from a sentence to a 256×256 photo failed. Stage-I generates a crude 64×64 image with rough shapes and colors from the caption. Stage-II takes *that* image plus the caption again and refines it to 256×256, fixing defects and adding detail. A "conditioning augmentation" trick adds noise to the text embedding so the model doesn't overfit to the small set of captions.<br><br>**Why it matters:** The first text-to-image results that were recognizably photographic, and the paper that established coarse-to-fine, multi-stage generation as the way to get resolution. Trained on narrow domains — birds and flowers — which is exactly why it worked and exactly what limited it.<br><br>**Artists:** Little gallery use; the domain restriction (you could have any bird you liked) made it more a research milestone than a tool.<br><br>**More examples:** [*StackGAN* (arXiv 1612.03242)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1612.03242) · [Code and samples](https://github.com/hanzhanggit/StackGAN)<br><br>**Try it:** No maintained demo; the [StackGAN-v2 repo](https://github.com/hanzhanggit/StackGAN-v2) still runs with older PyTorch. |

## Era 3 — GANs get good (2017–2019)

Resolution climbs from 64 pixels to a megapixel, control mechanisms multiply, and the first fully-formed AI art scene appears.

| Example | Model |
|---|---|
| ![[cyclegan-teaser.jpg\|340]] | **2017 · CycleGAN**<br><br>**Who:** Jun-Yan Zhu, Taesung Park, Phillip Isola, and Alexei A. Efros (UC Berkeley / BAIR).<br><br>**How it works:** pix2pix without paired data. You have a pile of horse photos and a pile of zebra photos, but no matched pairs. CycleGAN trains two generators — horse→zebra and zebra→horse — and adds a **cycle consistency loss**: translate a horse to a zebra and back, and you must recover the original horse. That round-trip constraint is what prevents the generator from ignoring the input and producing a generic zebra.<br><br>**Why it matters:** Paired data is expensive and often impossible — nobody has photographs of the same scene painted by Monet. CycleGAN removed that requirement and made style-domain translation available to anyone who could assemble two folders of images. This is what put custom, hand-curated datasets in reach of individual artists.<br><br>**Artists:** **Helena Sarin** (whose entire practice is small, personally photographed datasets run through CycleGAN and pix2pix, often printed or made into books), **Sofia Crespo**, **Anna Ridler**, **Nao Tokui**, **Derrick Schultz** (whose *Artificial Images* teaching materials trained much of the community).<br><br>**More examples:** [CycleGAN project page](https://junyanz.github.io/CycleGAN/) · [Helena Sarin's work](https://www.instagram.com/neuralbricolage/)<br><br>**Try it:** [TensorFlow CycleGAN tutorial (Colab)](https://colab.research.google.com/github/tensorflow/docs/blob/master/site/en/tutorials/generative/cyclegan.ipynb) · [Official PyTorch implementation](https://github.com/junyanz/pytorch-CycleGAN-and-pix2pix) |
| ![[progan-teaser.png\|340]] | **2017 · Progressive Growing of GANs (ProGAN)**<br><br>**Who:** Tero Karras, Timo Aila, Samuli Laine, and Jaakko Lehtinen (NVIDIA Research).<br><br>**How it works:** Don't train a high-resolution GAN — grow one. Start with a generator and discriminator that work at 4×4 pixels and train to convergence. Then fade in a new layer pair and train at 8×8. Then 16×16, and so on up to 1024×1024. Each stage inherits a stable model from the last, so the network learns global structure before it ever worries about pores and eyelashes.<br><br>**Why it matters:** The first megapixel GAN, and the first synthetic faces that were genuinely hard to identify as synthetic. It also introduced the CelebA-HQ dataset. This is the moment the deepfake conversation became urgent.<br><br>**Artists:** Widely adopted for large-format print work where resolution mattered; superseded within a year by StyleGAN from the same lab.<br><br>**More examples:** [*Progressive Growing of GANs* (arXiv 1710.10196)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1710.10196) · [NVIDIA's result videos](https://github.com/tkarras/progressive_growing_of_gans)<br><br>**Try it:** Superseded — use the StyleGAN demos below. |
| ![[attngan-bird.png\|340]] | **2017 / 2018 · AttnGAN**<br><br>**Who:** Tao Xu, Pengchuan Zhang, Qiuyuan Huang, Han Zhang, Zhe Gan, Xiaolei Huang, and Xiaodong He (Lehigh, Rutgers, Microsoft Research, Duke).<br><br>**How it works:** StackGAN plus **word-level attention**. Rather than compressing the caption into one embedding, AttnGAN keeps every word vector and lets each region of the image being generated attend to the words most relevant to it — the patch that will become a beak attends to "beak." It adds a DAMSM loss (Deep Attentional Multimodal Similarity Model) that scores how well the generated image matches the caption at the level of individual words, not just the sentence.<br><br>**Why it matters:** The best text-to-image model of the pre-CLIP era, and the clearest demonstration that fine-grained text grounding — not just more parameters — is what makes prompts actually steer the image. Attention over words is now universal.<br><br>**Artists:** Rarely used for finished work; like StackGAN it was confined to birds and COCO categories. But AttnGAN demos were many people's first experience of typing a sentence and getting a picture.<br><br>**More examples:** [*AttnGAN* (arXiv 1711.10485)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.10485) · [Microsoft's code and samples](https://github.com/taoxugit/AttnGAN)<br><br>**Try it:** [Runway and Hugging Face community reimplementations](https://huggingface.co/models?search=attngan); the original repo needs Python 2. |
| ![[biggan-samples.png\|340]] | **2018 · BigGAN**<br><br>**Who:** Andrew Brock, Jeff Donahue, and Karen Simonyan (DeepMind / Heriot-Watt University).<br><br>**How it works:** Mostly *scale*, applied carefully. ## Era 4 — Diffusion and language (2020–2022)

| ![[ddpm-celebahq.png\|340]]                                                        | **2020 · DDPM (Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models)**<br><br>**Who:** Jonathan Ho, Ajay Jain, and Pieter Abbeel (UC Berkeley).<br><br>**How it works:** The 2015 diffusion idea, made to work. The key reformulation: instead of training the network to predict the slightly-less-noisy image at each reverse step, train it to predict **the noise that was added**. That turns the objective into a simple weighted denoising loss, which is stable and easy to optimize. Combine with a U-Net backbone, a sinusoidal embedding telling the network which timestep it is on, and a well-chosen noise schedule, and diffusion suddenly matches GANs on sample quality.<br><br>**Why it matters:** The hinge of the entire timeline. Diffusion has properties GANs never had: training is stable (no adversarial collapse, no mode dropping), the model covers the whole data distribution, and generation is an *iterative* process you can interrupt, guide, and condition at every step. That last property is why every control mechanism from 2022 onward — inpainting, img2img, ControlNet — is easy in diffusion and was hard in GANs.<br><br>**Artists:** Not directly in 2020 — DDPM was unconditional and slow. Within eighteen months it was the substrate of everything.<br><br>**More examples:** [DDPM project page](https://hojonathanho.github.io/diffusion/) · [*Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models* (arXiv 2006.11239)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.11239) · [Lilian Weng's explainer](https://lilianweng.github.io/posts/2021-07-11-diffusion-models/)<br><br>**Try it:** [Hugging Face Diffusers "Training a diffusion model from scratch" (Colab)](https://colab.research.google.com/github/huggingface/diffusion-models-class/blob/main/unit1/01_introduction_to_diffusers.ipynb)                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                             |

First a discrete VAE compresses each 256×256 image into a grid of 32×32 tokens drawn from a codebook of 8192 possible image-patch symbols. Then a 12-billion-parameter GPT-style transformer is trained on sequences of *text tokens followed by image tokens*, on 250 million image-caption pairs from the web. To generate, you feed in the caption and let the transformer autoregressively write out 1024 image tokens, which the decoder turns back into pixels. Many candidates are generated and CLIP re-ranks them.<br><br>**Why it matters:** The first model that handled genuinely compositional, absurd prompts — *"an armchair in the shape of an avocado,"* *"a daikon radish in a tutu walking a dog"* — with obvious understanding of how to combine unrelated concepts. | ![[vqgan-clip-valley.jpg\|340]]                                                    | **2021 · CLIP-guided synthesis (VQGAN+CLIP, Disco Diffusion)**<br><br>**Who:** **CLIP** by Alec Radford and colleagues (OpenAI); **VQGAN** by Patrick Esser, Robin Rombach, and Björn Ommer (Heidelberg); the *combination* by independent researchers and artists — **Ryan Murdock** (*The Big Sleep*, BigGAN+CLIP) and **Katherine Crowson** (VQGAN+CLIP, and much of the later diffusion tooling), with **Disco Diffusion** assembled by *Somnai* and a large open collaboration.<br><br>**How it works:** CLIP is not a generator — it is a pair of encoders, one for images and one for text, trained on 400 million web pairs to map a picture and its caption to the same point in a shared space. The base model stops being the story. Everything interesting happens in how you constrain, personalize, and accelerate it.

| ![[dreambooth-teaser.jpg\|340]] | **2022 · DreamBooth, Textual Inversion, and LoRA**<br><br>**Who:** **DreamBooth**: Nataniel Ruiz, Yuanzhen Li, Varun Jampani, Yael Pritch, Michael Rubinstein, Kfir Aberman (Google Research and Boston University). **Textual Inversion**: Rinon Gal and colleagues (Tel Aviv University and NVIDIA). **LoRA**: Edward Hu and colleagues (Microsoft, 2021 — originally for language models, adapted to diffusion by the open-source community).<br><br>**How it works:** Three ways to teach an existing model one new thing from a handful of images. **Textual Inversion** freezes the entire model and learns a single new *word embedding* — a few hundred numbers — that points at your concept. **DreamBooth** fine-tunes the whole model on 3–5 photos bound to a rare token identifier, with a "prior preservation" loss so it doesn't forget what the general category looks like. **LoRA** trains small low-rank matrices that are added to the existing weights, producing an adapter file of a few megabytes that can be swapped in and out and stacked.<br><br>**Why it matters:** This is where generation became *personal*. Before this you could only ask for things the base model already knew. After it, artists could train a model on their own drawings, their own photographs, their own hand-built world, in an afternoon on a rented GPU. LoRA in particular — small, shareable, combinable — turned model customization into a folk practice with tens of thousands of community-published adapters.<br><br>**Artists:** Overwhelmingly used by illustrators and studios to encode a consistent character or house style; also the technical basis for both the most interesting artist-owned-dataset work and the most contested style-mimicry.<br><br>**More examples:** [DreamBooth project page](https://dreambooth.github.io/) · [Textual Inversion project page](https://textual-inversion.github.io/) · [Civitai model community](https://civitai.com/)<br><br>**Try it:** [Hugging Face DreamBooth training Space](https://huggingface.co/spaces/multimodalart/dreambooth-training) · [Diffusers LoRA training guide](https://huggingface.co/docs/diffusers/training/lora) |
| ![[controlnet-example.png\|340]] | **February 2023 · ControlNet**<br><br>**Who:** Lvmin Zhang, Anyi Rao, and Maneesh Agrawala (Stanford University).<br><br>**How it works:** Take a trained diffusion model and *clone* its encoder. Freeze the original copy; train the clone to accept an extra conditioning image — a pose skeleton, a depth map, a Canny edge detection, a scribble, a segmentation map, a normal map. The clone's outputs are added back into the frozen model through **zero-convolution** layers, which start at zero and therefore contribute nothing at initialization. That means training can never damage the base model, and a usable ControlNet can be trained on a single GPU from tens of thousands of examples rather than millions.<br><br>**Why it matters:** The control problem, solved. Prompts specify *what*; ControlNet specifies *where*. You can draw a stick figure and get a photograph in exactly that pose, or trace a photo's edges and re-render it in a new style with the composition preserved. For anyone with drawing skills this is what turned diffusion from a slot machine into a medium — you can compose, and the model renders. It is also the direct descendant of GauGAN's promise, generalized to arbitrary conditioning signals.<br><br>**Artists:** Near-universal in professional workflows — storyboarding, character consistency, architectural visualization, animation. It is the single most common reason a working illustrator uses a diffusion model at all.<br><br>**More examples:** [ControlNet repo with example galleries](https://github.com/lllyasviel/ControlNet) · [*Adding Conditional Control to Text-to-Image Diffusion Models* (arXiv 2302.05543)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.05543)<br><br>**Try it:** [ControlNet v1.1 Space on Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/spaces/hysts/ControlNet-v1-1) · built into [ComfyUI](https://github.com/comfyanonymous/ComfyUI) and [Krita AI Diffusion](https://github.com/Acly/krita-ai-diffusion) |
| ![[sdxl-astronaut.jpg\|340]]<br>![[sdxl-samples.jpg\|340]] | **July 2023 · SDXL, and the distillation race**<br><br>**Who:** **SDXL**: Dustin Podell, Zion English, Kyle Lacey, Andreas Blattmann, Tim Dockhorn, Jonas Müller, Joe Penna, Robin Rombach (Stability AI). **Latent Consistency Models**: Simian Luo and colleagues (Tsinghua). **SDXL-Turbo / adversarial diffusion distillation**: Axel Sauer and colleagues (Stability AI).<br><br>**How it works:** SDXL is latent diffusion scaled up and cleaned up — a 2.6-billion-parameter U-Net, *two* text encoders working together, native 1024×1024 output, a separate refiner model for final detail, and explicit conditioning on image size and crop coordinates so the model stops producing accidentally cropped subjects. You can paint and watch the render update as you move the brush. Latency is an aesthetic property.<br><br>**Artists:** Real-time diffusion produced a whole genre of live performance and installation work — canvas-and-cursor tools where the image updates continuously, and live-visuals systems driven by audio or camera input.<br><br>**More examples:** [*SDXL* (arXiv 2307.01952)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.01952) · [Stability's SDXL announcement](https://stability.ai/news/stable-diffusion-sdxl-1-announcement)<br><br>**Try it:** [SDXL Space on Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/spaces/google/sdxl) · [Latent Consistency Model real-time Space](https://huggingface.co/spaces/latent-consistency/lcm-lora-for-sdxl) |
| ![[flux1-valley.jpg\|340]]<br>![[sd35-astronaut.jpg\|340]] | **2024 · Diffusion Transformers and rectified flow (SD3, FLUX.1)**<br><br>**Who:** **DiT**: William Peebles and Saining Xie (UC Berkeley and NYU, 2022). **Stable Diffusion 3**: Patrick Esser, Sumith Kulal, Andreas Blattmann, Rahim Entezari and colleagues (Stability AI). **FLUX.1**: Black Forest Labs, founded by Robin Rombach, Andreas Blattmann, and Patrick Esser — the original Stable Diffusion authors.<br><br>**How it works:** Two changes at once. First, the U-Net is replaced by a **transformer** operating on latent patches, which scales more predictably and lets image and text tokens attend to each other in a single joint stream rather than through bolted-on cross-attention. Second, the noise schedule is replaced by **rectified flow**: instead of a curved, hand-designed diffusion trajectory, train the model to follow a straight line from noise to data, which is both simpler to reason about and much cheaper to sample.<br><br>**Why it matters:** This is the current architecture. It also finally solved the field's most conspicuous long-standing failure — **legible text inside images**. From alignDRAW in 2015 through SDXL in 2023, every model produced convincing typographic gibberish; joint text-image attention at scale is what fixed it. FLUX.1 [schnell] and [dev] also continued the open-weights tradition when Stability's position wavered.<br><br>**Artists:** The current default tools. FLUX in particular became the community's preferred open base model through 2024–2025, with its own ecosystem of LoRAs and ControlNets.<br><br>**More examples:** [*Scalable Diffusion Models with Transformers* (arXiv 2212.09748)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.09748) · [*Scaling Rectified Flow Transformers* (SD3, arXiv 2403.03206)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.03206) · [Black Forest Labs](https://blackforestlabs.ai/)<br><br>**Try it:** [FLUX.1 [schnell] Space on Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/spaces/black-forest-labs/FLUX.1-schnell) · [FLUX.1 Kontext (image editing) Space](https://huggingface.co/spaces/black-forest-labs/FLUX.1-Kontext-Dev) · [SD 3.5 Large Space](https://huggingface.co/spaces/stabilityai/stable-diffusion-3.5-large) |

## Era 6 — Images inside language models (2025–present)

The image model stops being a separate thing you call. It becomes a mode of the same model you talk to.

| Example | Model |
|---|---|
| ![[gpt-image-1-stop-sign.jpg\|340]]<br>![[gemini-image.jpg\|340]] | **2025–2026 · Natively multimodal image generation**<br><br>**Who:** OpenAI (4o image generation and the GPT Image models), Google DeepMind (Gemini's native image generation, known in its 2025 iteration by the nickname *nano banana*), and comparable systems from Black Forest Labs, ByteDance, and others.<br><br>**How it works:** Rather than a text encoder handing a prompt to a separate image model, a single multimodal transformer produces image tokens in the same autoregressive stream as its text tokens — often with a diffusion decoder for final pixel quality. Because it is the *same* model doing the reasoning and the drawing, it brings its full world knowledge and its conversational memory to bear: it can follow multi-turn instructions ("now make it evening, keep her jacket the same"), maintain a character across many images, read and rewrite text inside an image, and reason about what a diagram should contain before drawing it.<br><br>**Why it matters:** This closes the loop the field opened in 2015. alignDRAW's "a stop sign is flying in blue skies" was a smudge produced by a model that could not be argued with; the image on the left is the same prompt, rendered by a model you can converse with about the result. The interface shifts from **prompting** to **directing** — the unit of work is no longer a prompt but an editing conversation. Practically, this is also what made image generation ubiquitous rather than specialist: it lives inside the chat window people already have open.<br><br>**Artists:** Ubiquitous and largely invisible. The interesting artistic work at this point tends to be *about* these systems — the aesthetics of consensus, the flattening of visual culture, provenance and consent — rather than simply made with them. **Holly Herndon and Mat Dryhurst**, **Trevor Paglen**, **Stephanie Dinkins**, **Sougwen Chung**, and **Memo Akten** all work on that ground. Meanwhile, artists in the earlier rows of this table increasingly use *older*, more constrained models on purpose, for the same reasons a printmaker chooses a press.<br><br>**More examples:** [OpenAI: Introducing 4o image generation](https://openai.com/index/introducing-4o-image-generation/) · [Google DeepMind: Gemini image models](https://deepmind.google/models/gemini-image/) · [Wikimedia Commons: GPT Image and Gemini samples](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Images_created_with_artificial_intelligence)<br><br>**Try it:** [ChatGPT](https://chatgpt.com/) · [Google Gemini](https://gemini.google.com/) · [Google AI Studio](https://aistudio.google.com/) for API-level access |

## A note on the images on this page

Where possible the examples are the actual figures from the original papers, or images released by the model authors, so you are looking at what the technique genuinely produced at the time rather than a modern reconstruction. Sources: Wikimedia Commons (variously public domain, CC0, CC BY, and CC BY-SA — see each file's page for its terms), official project repositories and pages for pix2pix, CycleGAN, SPADE, StyleGAN, ControlNet, DreamBooth, Latent Diffusion, and Stable Diffusion, and figures extracted from the arXiv preprints for GAN, DRAW, alignDRAW, VAE, StackGAN, and the 2015 diffusion paper. All are reproduced here for classroom use with attribution to their original authors.

Several of the "same prompt, different model" comparisons — the astronaut riding a horse, the scenic valley, the flying stop sign — come from Wikimedia contributors who have deliberately run one prompt through successive generations of models. They are worth browsing as a set: [Category:Images created with artificial intelligence](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Images_created_with_artificial_intelligence).
